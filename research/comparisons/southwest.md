## 1. Summary

Southwest **confirms** two of the framework's ideas as long-running practice. One is price fairness as a public promise: free bags, no change fees, credits that never expire, and a 2015 campaign against hidden fees. The other is employees first, with profit sharing since the early 1970s and no involuntary layoffs for 53 years.

It is the **cautionary case** for FD-01 and FD-04: the promises were reversed within about 18 months after an activist investor took about 11 % and reshaped the board. A public analysis that justified free bags (September 2024) was replaced six months later by an unpublished one that justified fees. Customers and employees had no formal say. The investor sold down after the share price rose; the strategy stayed.

Lesson: price promises and employee commitments need anchoring in ownership, statutes or binding commitments to customers. A brand isn't enough.

## 3. Practices in detail

### SWA-01: "Transfarency"

- **What the company does:** a 2015 campaign promising "no unexpected bag fees, change fees, or hidden fees", on top of free bags since the founding years [S11, S12].
- **Fit:** match with principle 3 in spirit: fair, simple, explained prices.

### SWA-02: Promises reversed

- **What the company does:** at the September 2024 investor day, Southwest showed bag fees would earn $1–1.5B but lose $1.8B of market share, and kept "bags fly free". In March 2025 Jordan said: "in contrast to our previous analysis, actual customer booking behavior… did not show that we are getting the same benefit from our bundled offering with free bags." The new analysis wasn't published [S18, S21, S22].
- **Fit:** differs from principles 1 and 3. A price promise replaced without public evidence.
- **Candidate for rejection:** changing a public price commitment without publishing the analysis and giving customers a say.

### SWA-03: People first

- **What the company does:** Kelleher: "Your people come first, and if you treat them right, they'll treat the customers right, and the customers will come back, and that'll make the shareholders happy." [S66]
- **Fit:** gap. FD-02 and FD-07: a stated order of stakeholders. The framework names stakeholders but doesn't rank them.

### SWA-04: Profit sharing

- **What the company does:** the first airline to share profits with employees (early 1970s). It paid $667M for 2019, but $97M for 2025 against $2.9B returned to shareholders [S23, S27, S51].
- **Fit:** partial. A mechanism without a fixed ratio to shareholder payouts. Compare Buffer's fixed 8–15 % (BUF-07).

### SWA-05: No-layoff record

- **What the company does:** no involuntary layoffs through 9/11 and COVID. It ended in February 2025 with about 1,750 corporate jobs, and about 75 more were cut in May 2026 [S20, S25, S29].
- **Fit:** gap. FD-02: an employment commitment held by culture, not written down.

### SWA-06: Activist settlement

- **What the company does:** Elliott disclosed $1.9B (about 11 %) in June 2024. The settlement added five Elliott nominees and one further new director, a new chair, and Kelly's early exit. By April 2026 Elliott's directly held shares were below 5 %, but its economic exposure was about 8.5 %; three Elliott directors remained; the strategy stayed (corrected 2026-11-24) [S6, S8, S17, S19].
- **Fit:** gap. The clearest FD-01 evidence: board control, not a majority, was enough to reverse decades of principles.

### SWA-07: Research instead of input

- **What the company does:** the changes were justified by company research (80 % prefer assigned seating; the metasearch data). None of it was published, and no customer input mechanism exists [S13, S21].
- **Fit:** partial. Customers' views were used, not heard.

### SWA-08: Penalty as compensation

- **What the company does:** after the 2022 meltdown, most of DOT's record $140M penalty went to customers as $75 vouchers and points, plus more than $600M in refunds [S10].
- **Fit:** partial. Accountability flowing back to customers.

### SWA-09: Third-party measures

- **What the company does:** DOT complaint rates, ACSI and J.D. Power are published and show the trend. Southwest still has the lowest complaints, but ACSI fell from 80 to 77, with "trade-offs… between monetization and trust" [S44, S45, S56, S57].
- **Fit:** partial. Outside measures that the company doesn't control. Useful for FD-06.

### SWA-10: Unionised, well-paid workforce

- **What the company does:** about 84 % unionised. 2024 contracts gave pilots +29 % immediately and flight attendants +22 % [S47, S49, S50].
- **Fit:** gap. Employee voice through unions protected pay, but not jobs or the model.

### SWA-11: Shareholder returns during the transformation

- **What the company does:** $2.9B returned in 2025 and over $1.3B in Q1 2026, while employee profit sharing was $97M [S23, S33].
- **Fit:** gap. FD-01 and FD-07: where gains go.

### SWA-12: Loyalty and credits worsened

- **What the company does:**
  - Credits now expire after 12 months, or 6 for Basic fares.
  - Fewer points on cheap fares.
  - Companion Pass rules tightened from 2027.
  - A customer-of-size policy change.

  [S22, S42, S43]
- **Fit:** gap. Value taken from existing customers.

## 4. Where the framework goes further than this company

- **Cost disclosure:** none.
- **Public roadmap and open project management:** none.
- **Community input:** none. Customers were researched, not involved.

## 5. Candidates for explicit rejection

- **SWA-02:** reversing a public price promise without published evidence. Reject.
- **SWA-06 / SWA-11:** principles left unprotected from board control; gains to shareholders during cuts. Reject.

## 6. Open questions for the decisions

- **FD-01:** would a golden share, a foundation owner or a customer-elected board seat have stopped the reversal?
- **FD-04:** should the framework require that any change to a public price promise is announced with the full analysis and a consultation period?
- **FD-02:** should profit sharing be tied to shareholder payouts (a fixed ratio)?
