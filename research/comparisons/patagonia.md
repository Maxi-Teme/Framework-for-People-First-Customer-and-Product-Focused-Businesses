## 1. Summary

Patagonia **adds** the piece the framework most clearly lacks: a structure that locks the mission in after the founder. Voting control sits in a purpose trust and economic rights in a nonprofit, on top of a benefit-corporation charter. It also adds the strongest evidence so far for the environmental topic: 1 % of sales given for decades, product decisions driven by impact, repair over replacement, and impact reports that admit failures.

It **matches none** of the framework's 4 principles outright. There's no community-driven development and no open project management, and it **differs** from principle 3: it publishes no finances, costs or price breakdowns. Patagonia is a mission company that is open about its effects, not an open company. That distinction matters for Max's framework: being open and being good overlap, but they aren't the same thing.

## 3. Practices in detail

### PAT-01: Steward ownership

- **What Patagonia does:** Since 2022 all voting stock (about 2 %) is held by the Patagonia Purpose Trust and all non-voting stock (about 98 %) by the Holdfast Collective; profits not reinvested go to Holdfast for environmental work, $180M by November 2025 [1, 5, 7].
- **Why it works for them:** A founder willing to give up the economic value; a profitable company; a clear mission.
- **Fit:** gap. The framework has no ownership principle.
- **Transfer:** The strongest model so far for FD-01: separate control (bound to the purpose) from economic rights. Smaller versions exist (steward-ownership structures in Germany, for example the "Verantwortungseigentum" movement); worth a later study.

### PAT-02: Control and visibility of the structure

- **What Patagonia does:** The family keeps control through the trust; Holdfast as a 501(c)(4) can fund politics; about $20M of 2024 grants passed through a community foundation whose onward grants aren't visible; gift tax about $17M against an estimated $1.2B if given outright [5, 11].
- **Fit:** partial. Ownership is public in outline but the trust's governance and Holdfast's onward spending are not fully open.
- **Candidate to adapt:** if the framework adopts steward ownership, it should also require that the trust's rules and the use of the money are public.

### PAT-03: 1 % of sales, profit or not

- **What Patagonia does:** Since the 1980s; FY25: $14.7M to 824 nonprofits [2, 8].
- **Fit:** gap.
- **Transfer:** Tied to sales, so it doesn't vanish in bad years (FD-08). Compare Buffer's profit sharing (BUF-07) and Everlane's Black Friday Fund (EVL-05).

### PAT-04: Product decisions by impact

- **What Patagonia does:** In the early 1970s Chouinard shrank the piton business, his largest line, because pitons damaged rock, and pushed chocks instead. Later: organic cotton from 1996, recycled polyester from 1993, PFAS phase-out (99 % by weight by autumn 2024), no corporate logos on clothing [1, 2, 10].
- **Why it works for them:** Founder authority plus customers who share the values; the chocks sold out.
- **Fit:** gap. The framework's product development is driven by community input, not by impact.
- **Transfer:** FD-05 (who decides, and on what grounds) and FD-08 (impact as a decision criterion).

### PAT-05: Buy less, repair more

- **What Patagonia does:** "Don't Buy This Jacket" (2011) with the Common Threads pledge (reduce, repair, reuse, recycle, reimagine); Worn Wear resale; 174,799 repairs in FY25 [3, 8].
- **Fit:** gap.
- **Transfer:** A fairness practice similar to Buffer's cancelling of unused subscriptions (BUF-15): don't sell people what they don't need (FD-04, FD-08).

### PAT-06: Footprint and impact reporting

- **What Patagonia does:** Since 2007 it has published its footprint, "the good and the bad", and supplier information: more than 90 % of products made in Fair Trade Certified factories; 34 % of apparel-assembly factories paying a living wage on average (2023); FY25 emissions; missed targets and the dropped carbon-neutrality approach [2, 8, 9].
- **Fit:** partial. Deep transparency, but about impact rather than governance, development or prices.
- **Transfer:** Strong model for FD-03 (bad news published), FD-06 (public targets and admitted misses) and FD-08.

### PAT-07: No financial or price transparency

- **What Patagonia does:** Private; no published financial statements (revenue only estimated); no cost breakdowns of prices [1].
- **Fit:** differs from principles 1 and 3.
- **Candidate for explicit rejection:** a values company that keeps its finances and prices closed. Buffer shows both can be open (BUF-04, BUF-06).

### PAT-08: Employee benefits from early on

- **What Patagonia does:** On-site childcare (around 1984), flextime and the "let my people go surfing" culture [2, 4].
- **Fit:** gap.
- **Transfer:** FD-02, alongside Toyota's respect for people (TOY-10) and Buffer's pay and profit sharing (BUF-03, BUF-07).

### PAT-09: Labour relations under pressure

- **What Patagonia does:** In 2024, 90 remote customer-service staff were told to relocate or take severance, and 41 HQ roles were cut with at least 22 weeks' pay [6]. Reno (2024) and SoHo (2026) store workers unionised after benefits were cut; Patagonia didn't voluntarily recognise the unions but says it supports workers' right to vote [12, 13].
- **Fit:** partial. Better handled than Everlane (EVL-07), but employees still had no voice before the cuts.
- **Transfer:** FD-02: how employees take part in decisions that affect them.

### PAT-10: Fair Trade sewing and supplier labour standards

- **What Patagonia does:** Fair Trade Certified sewing since 2014; labour-broker standards after audits found trafficking in Taiwanese second-tier suppliers (2007, 2011) [1, 2, 9]. But it shares factories with fast-fashion brands where conditions fall short [1].
- **Fit:** gap.
- **Transfer:** FD-02 and FD-08: publish supplier standards and the gaps.

### PAT-11: Activism

- **What Patagonia does:** Sued the US government over national monuments (2017), led a trade-show boycott, put voting messages on labels, joined an advertising boycott [1].
- **Fit:** gap. Outside the framework's scope.
- **Transfer:** Note only. It may matter later if the framework takes a position on political activity, as Holdfast's political spending and Ben & Jerry's dispute (CS-014) show.

### PAT-12: Slower growth after the 1991 crisis

- **What Patagonia does:** After rapid growth in the 1980s, a recession and a withdrawn credit line forced it to lay off 20 % of staff in 1991; it then grew more slowly [2, 4].
- **Fit:** gap.
- **Transfer:** Same pattern as Buffer's 2016 crisis (BUF-12) and Toyota's 2010 lesson (TOY-13) (FD-07).

### PAT-13: Benefit corporation

- **What Patagonia does:** Since January 2012 a benefit corporation, the first in California, and a certified B Corp [2].
- **Fit:** gap.
- **Transfer:** A lighter way to write purpose into the legal form, available to small companies too (FD-01).

## 4. Where the framework goes further than Patagonia

- **Financial and price transparency:** none at Patagonia (PAT-07).
- **Community-driven product development:** Patagonia's community shares values and activism, but doesn't shape products in the open.
- **Open project management:** no public roadmap or boards.
- **Open governance:** the trust's rules and decisions aren't public.

## 5. Candidates for explicit rejection

Final decisions are in `../decisions.md`.

- PAT-07: closed finances and prices.
- PAT-02, the opaque parts only: trust governance and onward spending that aren't public.
- PAT-11: probably park rather than reject.

## 6. Open questions for the decisions

- Can a small company use steward ownership or a benefit-corporation form? What would that look like for a German sole proprietor or GmbH (FD-01)?
- If the framework adopts a purpose structure, what must it publish about the structure itself (FD-01, FD-03)?
- Should impact be a decision criterion for products alongside community input (FD-05, FD-08)?
- Should giving be tied to sales (Patagonia) or profit (Buffer) (FD-08, FD-02)?
