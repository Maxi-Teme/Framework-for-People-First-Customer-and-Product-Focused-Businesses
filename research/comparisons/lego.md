## 1. Summary

LEGO **confirms** the framework's community principle with the clearest process in this research. LEGO Ideas is open to anyone. It sets a public support threshold with time limits, runs fixed review rounds three times a year, and pays the fan designer a share of sales. In 15 years about 80 fan sets have gone on sale.

LEGO also **adds** two lessons the framework lacks:
- **Ownership:** a family holding and a foundation that receives a quarter of the dividends, with no outside investors.
- **Growth:** the 2004 crisis showed that diversification and complexity are growth traps.

It **differs** from the framework on pricing. There is no cost disclosure per product, and the 2022 price rises were explained only as "increased raw material and operating costs". Its Review Board also decides without publishing reasons. And where LEGO controls the community's own space (BrickLink, fan programmes), it has made abrupt changes and apologised afterwards.

## 3. Practices in detail

### LEG-01: LEGO Ideas

- **What the company does:** anyone can submit an idea. It needs 10,000 supporters to qualify, with time limits along the way (100 in 60 days, 1,000 within a further 365 days, and so on). There are three review rounds a year. A LEGO Review Board decides on criteria that include licensing, feasibility and expected demand "and much more". Results are announced publicly; reasons for rejection are not [52, 53, 54, 60].
- **Why it works for them:** a huge adult fan base, a modular product that fans can prototype themselves, and the commercial upside of tested demand.
- **Fit:** partial. It is the framework's "community-driven development" with a gate. It is closer to the framework than Buffer's upvote board (BUF-08) because the threshold is fixed and public. But the decision is closed: no scoring, no reasons.
- **Transfer:** a public threshold and fixed review dates suit a small company. Add what LEGO omits: publish the reason for each decision.

### LEG-02: Fan designers paid and credited

- **What the company does:** the Ideas designer receives "1 % of the total net sales" plus 10 copies, and is named on the box. "Net sales" is not defined publicly. The BrickLink Designer Program pays a sales share of 5 % or 10 %, depending on the source and version [52, 55, 70].
- **Fit:** gap. The framework doesn't say whether community contributors share in the value they create.
- **Transfer:** pay contributors whose ideas are built, with a published and clearly defined formula.

### LEG-03: Back to the core

- **What the company does:** after losses in 1998 and 2003–2004, LEGO cut costs by 20 %, sold the LEGOLAND parks and cut the number of unique components. Secondary sources say about 12,900 were reduced to 6,000–7,000 [39, 41, 42].
- **Fit:** gap. It is FD-07 evidence: diversification and complexity as growth traps. It is the same pattern as Patagonia in 1991 (PAT-12) and Buffer in 2016 (BUF-12).

### LEG-04: "Right to hack"

- **What the company does:** in 1998 hobbyists cracked the Mindstorms firmware. LEGO reportedly chose a licence clause allowing it, and later an SDK, instead of legal action. This comes from a secondary source citing Robertson [56b].
- **Fit:** gap, and close to Framework's open interfaces (FRM-09).
- **Transfer:** when users modify the product, treat it as product development, not a breach.

### LEG-05: Fan programmes

- **What the company does:** the Ambassador Network for recognised fan groups ran from about 2005. In August 2026 it was replaced by LEGO Fan CoLab, and all members had to re-apply [66, 67].
- **Fit:** partial. It gives community representatives a channel, but the company sets the terms and can reset them.

### LEG-06: Owning the fans' marketplace

- **What the company does:** LEGO bought BrickLink in 2019. In November 2025 it announced marketplace closures in 35 countries, affecting more than 100,000 members, and gave only "greater complications" as the reason. BrickLink's head then apologised: "We went about informing… the wrong way… It wasn't with the care and transparency we strive for." The deadline was extended and fees were waived. A cyber incident followed in November 2026 [68, 71, 72].
- **Fit:** partial. LEGO corrected course in public, but only after the backlash.
- **Transfer (FD-06):** when a company runs infrastructure its community depends on, changes need advance notice, reasons and a consultation period. Write that down before it's needed.

### LEG-07: Family and foundation ownership

- **What the company does:** LEGO Holding (KIRKBI, the family holding) owns 75 % and the LEGO Foundation 25 %. The Foundation receives 25 % of dividends: DKK 2,250m in 2025. It committed DKK 1,726m in grants that year. There are no outside investors [1, 9, 10].
- **Why it works for them:** four generations of family control, with a planned handover (completed in 2023), and enough profit to fund the Foundation.
- **Fit:** gap. It is FD-01 evidence alongside Patagonia (PAT-01) and Toyota (TOY-17), and comparable to the German foundation model (Bosch, Zeiss).
- **Transfer:** for a small company, the relevant part is a fixed, published share of profits for a purpose, written into the ownership structure.

### LEG-08: Trademark royalty outside the foundation split

- **What the company does:** the LEGO Group pays a trademark royalty to LEGO Holding: DKK 2,965m in 2025. This is in addition to dividends [1, 8].
- **Fit:** partial. It is disclosed, but it means the Foundation's effective share of LEGO's cash is probably below the headline 25 % (an inference, not a disclosed figure).
- **Transfer:** when a purpose share is promised, report it against all cash flowing to owners, not only dividends.

### LEG-09: No cost disclosure

- **What the company does:** there are no per-product costs. The 2022 increases on existing sets (mostly up to 25 %) were explained as "increased raw material and operating costs", while net profit rose 4 % that year. One analysis found licensed sets cost about 21 % more per piece [22, 23, 24, 27].
- **Fit:** differs from principle 3.
- **Candidate for rejection:** as with PAT-07 and GIT-09.

### LEG-10: Owner absorbs sustainability costs

- **What the company does:** Christiansen (2024): "With a family owner committed to sustainability, it's a privilege that we can pay extra for the raw materials without having to charge customers extra." [26]
- **Fit:** gap. It is a claim, unverified, because no cost data is published. It links ownership (FD-01) to pricing (FD-04).

### LEG-11: Sustainability reporting with contested accounting

- **What the company does:** it sets targets (half of materials from sustainable sources by 2026, fossil-free by 2032) and reports "52 % renewable/recycled" content through mass-balance accounting. LEGO itself says it can't guarantee the share in any single brick. It admitted publicly that the recycled-PET brick failed [2, 15, 26, 29, 30].
- **Fit:** partial. The reporting is honest about the failure, but the headline figure is an accounting construct.

### LEG-12: Supplier audits with published rates

- **What the company does:** suppliers in high-risk countries are audited each year. The share with higher-risk non-conformities fell from 35 % (2023) to 26 % (2024) and 20 % (2025). Critical findings were 4 in 2023 and 2 in 2024, all about excessive hours. The 2025 target was loosened without explanation, then reset [1, 19].
- **Fit:** partial (suppliers are only in the framework's glossary). It is a concrete measure for FD-08.

### LEG-13: Detailed annual reporting

- **What the company does:** although privately owned, LEGO publishes full financials, gross margin, employee motivation and turnover, safety, and sustainability KPIs every year [1].
- **Fit:** match with principle 1 at the level of the company, though not of products or prices.

### LEG-14: Outsourcing reversed

- **What the company does:** production was outsourced to Flextronics in 2006 and brought back in-house by 2008. LEGO has since built its own factories close to its markets [45, 46, 48, 49].
- **Fit:** gap. It is FD-07 evidence: control of quality and the long term over short-term cost.

## 4. Where the framework goes further than this company

- **Cost and price transparency (principle 3):** LEGO discloses none per product.
- **Open project management (principle 4):** there is no public roadmap; Ideas reviews are announced, but other product plans are secret.
- **Reasons for decisions:** the Review Board publishes outcomes, not reasons.

## 5. Candidates for explicit rejection

- **LEG-09, prices without cost disclosure:** reject, as with PAT-07.
- **LEG-01, part of it: a closed decision gate without reasons:** adapt. Keep the threshold and review rounds, and add published reasons.

## 6. Open questions for the decisions

- **FD-05:** threshold (LEGO), upvotes (Buffer) or open interfaces (Framework Computer)? Should the framework set one mechanism, or require any mechanism with public rules and published reasons?
- **FD-05 / FD-02:** should contributors whose ideas are built be paid, and how?
- **FD-06:** what notice and consultation does a company owe a community that depends on its platform?
