## 1. Summary

Everlane **confirms** the framework's third principle in its simplest form: a cost and markup breakdown for every product, and a pricing experiment where customers saw what each price covers. It **differs** from the framework twice: transparency was selective, a story about costs and factories but not about wages, audit results, finances or governance; and the company used "transparency" as an argument against its customer-service staff organising. It **adds** environmental commitments with deadlines, which the framework doesn't cover at all, so this comparison opens a new area (EN) and a new decision topic (FD-08).

The main lesson is about what's missing from the framework. Nothing in Everlane's ownership or governance protected its values. Capital came with growth expectations (venture capital, then private equity, then $90M of debt), the founder stepped back, and the owners sold to the brand's opposite. The transparency *claims* survived the sale; the substance didn't. This is the cautionary counterpart to Buffer (BUF-10, BUF-11) and the third company on most decision topics.

## 3. Practices in detail

### EVL-01: Cost and markup breakdown for every product

- **What Everlane does:** Shows materials, labour and transport behind each price, compared with a "traditional retail" price: a T-shirt costing about $6 sold for $15 against $45 elsewhere (2015); today $30 against $55 [2, 16].
- **Why it worked for them:** A clear, new message in a market where costs were hidden; cheap online advertising to spread it [2, 8].
- **Fit:** match with principle 3.
- **But:** competitors copied it, so it became "table stakes" [11]; and it survived as a claim on the website after the business behind it was sold [16].
- **Transfer:** Keep it, but tie it to verification and governance so it can't outlive its substance (FD-04, FD-03).

### EVL-02: Selective transparency used as a brand story

- **What Everlane does:** Costs and factory stories were public; wages, full audit results, a complete supplier list, finances and governance were not [2, 6]. The founder: "We didn't intend to be transparent about costs and factories, but we realized it was a real angle for us to tell the story." [2]
- **Why it worked for them:** It sold well while it was new.
- **Fit:** differs. The framework asks for transparency across governance, development and pricing, as accountability, not marketing.
- **Candidate for explicit rejection:** transparency chosen for what sells. The framework should define the minimum set that must be public (FD-03).

### EVL-03: Factory profiles and audit claims

- **What Everlane does:** Named factories with profiles and a stated audit goal of 90 or more; scores mentioned for some, full results not published; minimum wage required, not a living wage; no full or second-tier supplier list [6, 16].
- **Fit:** partial. Supplier transparency is in the framework's spirit but not in its text.
- **Transfer:** If suppliers are covered (FD-02, FD-08), say what is published: names, audit results, wages, and who audits.

### EVL-04: "Choose What You Pay"

- **What Everlane does:** In end-of-season sales each item had 3 prices: production and shipping; plus overhead; plus growth. In a pre-test about 10 % chose the middle or highest price [3].
- **Why it worked for them:** Cleared old stock and taught customers the cost structure.
- **Fit:** partial. Goes beyond principle 3 by letting customers choose with the cost logic in front of them.
- **Transfer:** A possible mechanism for FD-04. The pre-test also warns that disclosure alone doesn't make most people pay more.

### EVL-05: Black Friday Fund

- **What Everlane does:** Donates Black Friday profits to projects for factory workers [16].
- **Fit:** gap.
- **Transfer:** One way to share profit along the supply chain (FD-08); compare Buffer's profit sharing with its own team (BUF-07).

### EVL-06: Environmental commitments with public deadlines

- **What Everlane does:** No virgin plastic by 2021; organic cotton targets; science-based climate targets; it reported progress, e.g. about 90 % of new plastic removed by late 2021 [1, 6].
- **Fit:** gap. The framework has no environmental principle.
- **Transfer:** Public targets with deadlines are a strong mechanism (FD-06). Whether the framework covers environmental impact at all is a new question (FD-08).

### EVL-07: Response to a union drive

- **What Everlane does:** Customer-service staff organised (December 2019) and asked for recognition on 23 March 2020; 4 days later 42 of 57 were laid off. Everlane cited Covid-19; the union alleged union-busting, including company emails arguing unions could reduce transparency; charges were not pursued [4].
- **Fit:** differs. The framework wants stakeholders to "participate actively in shaping policies and practices".
- **Candidate for explicit rejection:** treating employee organisation as a threat, and using transparency as the argument. FD-02 should say how employees get a voice.

### EVL-08: High-valuation venture capital, then private equity and debt

- **What Everlane does:** Venture funding from 2011; about $250M valuation in 2016; $85M round led by L Catterton in 2020; $90M debt in 2022; L Catterton majority; sold for a reported $100M with nothing reported for common shareholders [5, 8, 9, 11].
- **Why it happened:** Each round set growth expectations that a slow, careful supply chain couldn't meet [11, 15].
- **Fit:** gap. The framework has no ownership principle.
- **Candidate for explicit rejection:** high-valuation equity with growth expectations and preferences for a values-led company. The founder's new brand rejects it too: "no venture capital, no private equity" [13].

### EVL-09: Values carried by the founder and the brand story

- **What Everlane does:** No charter, special share or owner commitment protected the values; the founder stepped back (2022), left the board (2026) and learned of the sale from the press [1, 13, 14].
- **Fit:** gap.
- **Transfer:** Same lesson as Buffer's BUF-11 from the other side: values need a structural lock-in that survives the founder (FD-01).

### EVL-10: Growth faster than the supply chain

- **What Everlane does:** Experts say growth was pushed faster than its slow-fashion supply chain allowed; customers reported thinner fabrics, more synthetics and inconsistent sizing [15].
- **Fit:** gap.
- **Candidate for explicit rejection:** growth that forces quality cuts. Supports Toyota's growth guardrail (TOY-13) and Buffer's calm growth (BUF-12) (FD-07).

### EVL-11: No discounts at launch, later regular sales

- **What Everlane does:** Launched with "no stores, no collections, no discounts" [2]; later stores, regular sales events and Amazon [1, 8, 16].
- **Fit:** gap. The framework doesn't address discounts.
- **Transfer:** If prices are fair and explained, frequent discounts undermine the explanation. A rule on discounts belongs in FD-04.

### EVL-12: Reactive transparency after a crisis

- **What Everlane does:** After the 2020 allegations, published workforce diversity figures and commitments [5, 6].
- **Fit:** partial.
- **Transfer:** Transparency that starts after a scandal is better than none, but the framework should make it the default (FD-03).

## 4. Where the framework goes further than Everlane

- **Transparency of governance and finances:** Everlane never published finances, ownership or board decisions. Buffer did (BUF-04, BUF-05, BUF-09).
- **Community-driven product development and open project management:** no evidence Everlane opened either.
- **Accountability:** the framework asks for scrutiny of decisions; Everlane's biggest decisions (the 2020 layoffs, the sale) were made without it.

## 5. Candidates for explicit rejection

Final decisions are in `../decisions.md`.

- EVL-02: transparency chosen for what sells.
- EVL-07: treating employee organisation as a threat, with transparency as the argument.
- EVL-08: high-valuation equity with growth expectations and preferences.
- EVL-10: growth that forces quality cuts.

## 6. Open questions for the decisions

- What is the minimum set that must be public, so transparency can't be reduced to marketing (FD-03)?
- Should cost breakdowns be checked by someone outside the company (FD-04)?
- Does the framework cover environmental and supply-chain impact (FD-08)?
- Which ownership structures would have stopped the sale to Shein (FD-01)?
