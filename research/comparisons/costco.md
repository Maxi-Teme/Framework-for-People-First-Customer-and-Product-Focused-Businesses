## 1. Summary

Costco **adds** the strongest alternative to cost disclosure in this research. It caps its margin on goods and makes about half its operating profit from a membership fee that the member can take back at any time. That aligns the company's interest with the customer's without publishing a single cost. It is also the strongest case for employees as part of the business model: high pay, promotion from within and 94 % retention, defended for two decades as "not altruism" but good business.

It **confirms** public accountability in one unusual form: monthly sales reports.

It **differs** from the framework on disclosure:
- the markup cap is an unwritten founder rule, not a published policy;
- there are no costs, no list of who makes its Kirkland products, and no item-level accounting;
- there is no formal channel for member input.

The 2026 tariff refunds show the risk. A price promise without a published rule leaves management to decide how much of a windfall reaches customers ("partial reinvestment").

## 3. Practices in detail

### COS-01: Markup ceiling

- **What the company does:** Sinegal's rule as reported by the NYT (2005): no branded item marked up more than 14 %, no private-label item more than 15 % [19, 20]. Another source gives a 12–15 % band [21]. The rule is in no filing, and no current executive has confirmed it [2, 21].
- **Why it works for them:** high volume, few products and fee income allow very low margins on goods. Gross margin is about 11 % [52].
- **Fit:** partial. It is a price *rule*, which the framework lacks, but an unpublished one.
- **Transfer:** a published margin cap is the simplest form of FD-04's "published rule". Unlike Costco, write it down.

### COS-02: Profit from fees, not goods

- **What the company does:** membership fees were 50.6 % of operating income in FY2026, from 2 % of revenue [1]. CEO Vachris: "Fee increases go back to the members in lower prices" [48].
- **Fit:** gap. It is a business model in which profit comes from a transparent, fixed fee, and goods are sold near cost.
- **Transfer:** for a small company, a subscription or membership fee that funds the business can make it possible to sell at cost and show the costs.

### COS-03: Symbolic prices

- **What the company does:** the $1.50 hot dog and soda combo since 1985. Jelinek tells how Sinegal answered his request to raise it: "If you raise the effing hot dog, I will kill you. Figure it out." Costco built its own hot-dog plants to keep the price. Every CEO since has reaffirmed it [15, 16, 17, 18].
- **Fit:** gap. A visible promise that makes the price principle believable, and forces the company to cut its own costs.

### COS-04: Few products, savings or nothing

- **What the company does:** about 4,000 items. Items "that cannot be purchased at prices low enough to pass along meaningful cost savings are often not carried" [22].
- **Fit:** gap. The product decision follows the price principle.

### COS-05: Refundable membership

- **What the company does:** "We will cancel and refund your membership fee at any time if you are dissatisfied." [36]
- **Fit:** gap. The customer can always leave and get the fee back. This is the strongest fairness guarantee in the archive, comparable to Buffer cancelling unused subscriptions (BUF-15).

### COS-06: Tariff refunds and "partial reinvestment"

- **What the company does:**
  - **March 2026:** Vachris promised to return the value of tariff refunds "through lower prices and better values" and to "be transparent in how we plan to do this".
  - **Q4 FY2026 release:** refunds counted as profit "less partial reinvestment of those refunds in increased member values", worth $0.15 per share.
  - **Lawsuits:** class actions argue that members who paid the tariff-inflated prices aren't repaid.

  [1, 40, 41, 42]
- **Fit:** partial. A promise was made in public, but there is no rule on how much goes back, and no published figure yet.
- **Transfer (FD-04):** this is the case Framework Computer handled with a rule set in advance (FRM-03). A windfall should be passed on by a published rule, not by management discretion.

### COS-07: Monthly sales reports

- **What the company does:** it publishes monthly sales with comparable figures by region, which most retailers have stopped doing [10, 55].
- **Fit:** match. It is a regular public measure, like Buffer's live metrics (BUF-04).

### COS-08: What isn't disclosed

- **What the company does:** no guidance ("obviously we don't give guidance"), no markup policy in filings, no item costs, no list of Kirkland makers, no margins on loss leaders [21, 52].
- **Fit:** differs from principle 3 and with FD-03's minimum public set.
- **Candidate for rejection:** as with PAT-07.

### COS-09: Pay and promotion as strategy

- **What the company does:**
  - **Pay:** average US hourly pay about $33; starting pay $20.
  - **Staffing and retention:** at least half of staff full-time; 94 % retention after a year.
  - **Promotion:** from within.
  - **Stated reason:** Jelinek to the Senate: "this isn't altruism… paying employees good wages… makes sense for our business".

  [2, 30, 35]
- **Why it works for them:** low turnover cuts training costs; experienced staff run a lean warehouse; few products make cross-training feasible (Ton) [47].
- **Fit:** gap. The strongest evidence for FD-02.
- **Transfer:** state pay as part of the model, with a published floor and a retention measure.

### COS-10: CEO pay limit eroded

- **What the company does:** Sinegal said paying a CEO "100 or 200 or 300 times more than the average person working on the floor is wrong", with a salary of $350,000. The FY2025 ratio is 283:1, or 210:1 for full-time staff only. Costco publishes that second figure voluntarily [24, 26].
- **Fit:** partial. A founder norm without a rule fades.
- **Transfer:** if the framework sets a pay ratio, write it down.

### COS-11: Unions

- **What the company does:** about 5 % of staff are represented by the Teamsters. After a warehouse voted to unionise in 2023, management wrote: "We're not anti-union… The fact that a majority of Norfolk employees felt that they wanted or needed a union constitutes a failure on our part." The 2025 contract came only after a strike authorisation [31, 32, 34].
- **Fit:** partial. More respectful than Everlane (EVL-07), but employee voice still runs mainly through management.

### COS-12: Vertical integration and animal welfare

- **What the company does:** it built a $450M poultry complex to keep the $4.99 chicken. An undercover investigation in 2021 and a shareholder suit (dismissed) followed. Animal-welfare audits rely mainly on suppliers, and Costco concedes that the number of producers limits audits [45, 46].
- **Fit:** partial. A price promise can push costs onto animals and suppliers. FD-08 should consider this.

### COS-13: Informal member feedback

- **What the company does:** changes follow member complaints. Examples: membership-only food courts ("We were getting member complaints"), and the unpopular calzone replaced. There is no member panel or published feedback process [38, 51].
- **Fit:** partial with principle 2: responsive, but without a mechanism.

### COS-14: Fairness to paying members

- **What the company does:** a card-sharing crackdown, entrance scanners and member-only food courts. Costco explains: "We don't feel it's right that non-members receive the same benefits and pricing as our members." [37, 38]
- **Fit:** gap. If members pay for low prices, the company has to protect what they paid for.

### COS-15: Long-term orientation, and special dividends

- **What the company does:** it defended wages against analyst criticism in 2004 ("At Costco, it's better to be an employee or a customer than a shareholder"), and pays no guidance-driven quarterly game. It has also paid six special dividends, the latest $30 a share (about $13.3B) in 2026 [7, 23].
- **Fit:** gap. Long-term orientation without protective ownership, held by culture and results.

## 4. Where the framework goes further than this company

- **Cost disclosure and a published price rule:** Costco has neither in writing.
- **Community-driven development and open project management:** Costco has no community product process and no public roadmap.
- **Open finances beyond legal requirements:** Costco gives monthly sales but no guidance or cost detail.

## 5. Candidates for explicit rejection

- **COS-08, closed costs and suppliers:** reject, as with PAT-07.
- **COS-01 in its unwritten form:** adapt. The cap is a strong idea; it must be published.
- **COS-06, discretionary pass-through of windfalls:** adapt into a published rule, as Framework Computer did (FRM-03).

## 6. Open questions for the decisions

- **FD-04:** published cost disclosure, a published margin cap, or a fee model? Can a small company combine Costco's cap with Framework's change log?
- **FD-02:** should the framework set a pay floor, a pay ratio or a retention measure, or only require that pay rules be published?
- **FD-05:** can a members' model (Costco) support a formal member-input channel the way LEGO Ideas does?
